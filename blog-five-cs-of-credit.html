The Five Cs of Credit: How Lenders Evaluate Commercial Borrowers

Banking & CreditBy GRC Training Online · November 26, 2026

The five Cs of credit are character, capacity, capital, collateral and conditions. They are the classic framework lenders use to decide whether to extend credit, how much, and on what terms. Here is what each one means in commercial lending, what a credit analyst looks at, and the red flags that weaken a request.

1. Character

Character is the borrower's willingness to repay and the trust a lender can place in management. It is the most judgment-based of the five Cs.

What lenders review: repayment history with the bank and other creditors, credit reports, management's experience in the industry, the quality and timeliness of financial reporting, references, and any litigation or regulatory history.

Red flags: late or inconsistent financial statements, unexplained changes in accountants, a history of covenant breaches, and answers that change from one meeting to the next.

2. Capacity

Capacity is the ability to repay the debt from the business's cash flow. For most commercial loans it is the primary source of repayment and the heart of the analysis.

What lenders review: historical and projected cash flow, and the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR): cash flow available for debt service divided by required principal and interest payments. Many lenders look for a DSCR comfortably above 1.0x, often 1.20x or higher, though thresholds vary by lender, industry and loan type.

Red flags: projections that are much stronger than past results, revenue concentrated in one or two customers, and cash flow that only covers debt service in the best case.

3. Capital

Capital is the borrower's own financial stake: equity in the business, and the cushion it provides if things go wrong.

What lenders review: balance-sheet strength, leverage (such as debt-to-worth or debt-to-EBITDA), retained earnings, and how much of the owner's own money is in the project or company.

Red flags: thin or negative equity, heavy distributions to owners while debt is rising, and a request where the borrower has little at risk.

4. Collateral

Collateral is the assets pledged to secure the loan, the lender's secondary source of repayment if cash flow fails.

What lenders review: the type and value of the assets (real estate, receivables, inventory, equipment), appraisals, loan-to-value, how easily the assets could be sold, lien priority, and personal or corporate guarantees.

Red flags: specialized assets with few buyers, out-of-date appraisals, and a credit request that relies on collateral because cash flow can't support the debt. Good collateral doesn't fix a weak repayment source.

5. Conditions

Conditions cover the purpose of the loan, its terms, and the outside environment the borrower operates in.

What lenders review: what the money is for, the structure and term of the loan, covenants, and economic, industry, interest-rate and regulatory factors that could affect the borrower's ability to repay.

Red flags: a loan purpose that doesn't match the term (short-term needs funded with long-term debt, or the reverse), and an industry facing structural decline.

How the five Cs work together

No single C decides a credit. A strong analysis weighs them together: capacity shows the loan can be repaid, capital shows the borrower can absorb a bad year, collateral protects the lender if things go wrong, character shows management will act in good faith, and conditions show whether the plan makes sense in the current environment. Weakness in one area can sometimes be offset by strength in another through structure, such as a lower advance rate, a guarantee or tighter covenants.

The framework also only works when it is applied consistently. That is where a bank's credit culture comes in: the shared standards and discipline that decide how exceptions are handled and how risk is monitored after the loan closes.

Frequently asked questions

What are the five Cs of credit?

Character, capacity, capital, collateral and conditions. Together they give lenders a structured way to judge whether a borrower is willing and able to repay, and what protects the lender if they don't.

Which of the five Cs matters most?

Capacity, the ability to repay from cash flow, is usually the primary consideration in commercial lending. Collateral is a secondary source of repayment, not a substitute for cash flow.

Is the five Cs framework only for commercial loans?

No. It is used in consumer, small-business and commercial lending, although the measures lenders use for each C differ by loan type.
